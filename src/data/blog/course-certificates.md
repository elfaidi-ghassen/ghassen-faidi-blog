You probably know the answer, which is... no. They don't matter much. Employers won't care. Build projects and gain experience, so on and so on. You've probably heard this before.

But I think that's not the complete picture. Certificates could be a really good source of motivation. Human beings tend to love collecting stuff. That's why kids love Pokémon, for instance, and why they love collecting badges in class. It's human nature.

After completing a long course, it feels nice to receive a certificate with your name on it. At least, it does for me. Maybe it's a weakness or some guilty pleasure, but I'm okay with that.

Some people don't care about that. They believe knowledge is worth pursuing in itself, and that the real joy is in learning and seeing yourself get better. Those people are cool! I'd love to be friends with them! But I'm not one of them.

So if having a certificate at the end of a course helps motivate you to complete a good or challenging course, then paying for that certificate might be worth it. The value isn't in the certificate itself. The value is in the motivation it provides.

I also found sharing certificates on LinkedIn to be a little bit helpful. To some extent, it says that you're at least putting some effort into your learning and that maybe you take it seriously.

Having said that, don't overdo it! I see no meaning in sharing too many certificates. And for some reason, the more courses I take, the less urge I feel to share them. It's funny remembering how happy I was when I completed the Systematic Program Design course (which took me around two months to complete) that I actually printed the certificate. But I'm glad such silly things make me happy.

And insights from learning theory explain this idea so well. It turns out that extrinsic and intrinsic motivation aren't necessarily opposites. Extrinsic means of motivation, like badges, certificates, or praise from a teacher, can have a great impact on a learner's motivation and give them a reason to continue.

At some point, the learner might not need the external motivation anymore because they've started to enjoy the material itself.

Barbara Oakley gives a sweet example of how she motivated her young daughter Rachel to start reading books. She did something simple: whenever Rachel completed a book, she would give her... money! Well, sometimes simple solutions work wonders.

Rachel wasn't used to reading books, so reading itself was painful. It wasn't a fun activity, but rewards made it bearable. The key idea, though, is that reading gets easier the more you read! At some point, it even becomes fun. And then Barbara simply stopped rewarding her. There was no need for it anymore. Rachel was already hooked on reading!

It makes so much sense when you think about it. So try to find external sources of motivation that work for you, and make use of them!
