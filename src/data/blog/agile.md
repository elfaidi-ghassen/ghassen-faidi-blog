I used to look down on Agile and such methodologies. It's so hard to be fair when you've been bombarded with buzzwords for quite a long time. Even a term like "iterative development" feels like a buzzword to me - even though it's objectively a useful idea (but honestly, try searching for "iterative" vs "incremental" vs "spiral"...[^incremental]). At some point it all starts to feel like boring management stuff.

 After doing some reading, I realized Agile wasn't that bad after all. It has some very cool ideas, and more importantly, it's a set of *values* and *practices* that you can apply. Like focusing on the human side of building software and having a good culture. It's not some rigid methodology that tells you exactly what to do and in which order.  Simply because "there is no silver bullet" in software. And the awesome people behind Agile knew that.

But in the end, it all boils down to how we implement Agile. It can differ, and it can go wrong - really fast (nope, not gonna make that joke).
But let's look at another field that also has some "best practices". Teaching.
## Checklist Teaching
Have you ever heard of teaching best practices? You can find rubrics and checklists online that help you become a "better teacher."
It's what Barbara Oakley often calls *checklist teaching*. Yet again, such approaches often fail.



For example, you probably have seen many teachers starting a lecture, whether in person or in an online course, by stating the objectives of the lecture and the expected outcomes of the lesson. It's often useless and boring.

As a student, I can quickly jump to the conclusion, "These teachers don't know how to teach. They are using outdated, stupid practices..."
But is that the full picture?

I could probably convince you that such things matter. Hear me out: "Teaching isn't just about dumping down information. It's about building rich and intricate connections in the student's brain. Starting off by explaining the objectives will guide the student's thinking in the right direction throughout the lecture, hence helping them build better connections and fully grasp the material. *And it just takes a couple of minutes... it can't hurt*. And best of all, the science supports this."

Then, it is a good practice based on good values. But it often doesn't work. Simply because the teacher thinks of it as a "checklist", just something to add to the lecture notes or the slides.
Another teacher might get the value behind the practice and could even implement it differently. They might start with a story or a real-life experience that shows the problem we're trying to solve by the end of the lecture. There are many opportunities to get creative. 
And I believe this is why teaching is *hard*. It requires a lot of human care and enthusiasm, and sadly, often in an environment that might [not have enough incentives](https://www.reddit.com/r/Teachers/comments/1lvncoc/how_annoying_is_typing_the_learning_objectives/). 

>We honestly waste so much time as educators trying to prove that we're doing our job that it actively interferes with learning.

>It's a tedious academic exercise that has been forced into a student-facing procedure. On an academic level, these are things that we as teachers should think about and consider when planning.

>We hate it.

Now doesn't that sound like a burned-out software engineer? 😅

And I'm just talking about actually "useful" practices that are based on both human experience and science. Some practices aren't even based on science, like helping your students develop a [Growth Mindset](https://www.wired.com/story/growth-mindset-education-psychological-theory-children-mirage/). But let's get back to Agile.
## Checklist Agile
So, things are not going well in the company. You want to "go agile", that is, you probably gonna add some more meetings[^going-agile], let's say a stand-up meeting. Sounds like a good idea, *and it just takes a couple of minutes... it can't hurt*.
But for some reason, the developers seem to hate it. They do attend the meeting, but at some point... they start saying the minimum number of words to get by. They want to get back to "real work". But why is this " best practice " failing!? It's the same reason checklist-teaching often fails. Not understanding the underlying value behind the practice, and blindly applying it.

In my country, we have an old wise saying that is loosely translated as: 

> Judging something is a reflection of one's perception of it.

How we perceive something can affect how motivated we are. For example, two people can perceive studying differently, and hence it can change how much they do it. It's part of being human. Thoughts can have a huge impact on our emotions and actions (and those themselves can have an impact on our thoughts). 

The problem with many meetings is that not everyone in the team sees the *value* in them. Developers, for instance, often find meetings annoying and take a lot of valuable time that could have been used for "real work": building actual software.
They perceive meetings as a chore. They think of it as yet another tool for the PM. Just some management stuff. That is, the PM wants to see if things are going well and if it's according to the plan. It has nothing to do with us. This ends up reducing the meeting's value. And that's how most stand-up meetings are. No wonder many people hate them.

If you can show your team that it can actually be useful for *them* by designing actually useful meetings: if the meeting becomes a planning tool for everyone, a meeting that makes sure everyone is taking good decisions (and not just following the plan), not just a mere status sync. The developers then *might* start to find it useful. If the developers start to think of it as a way to understand how the project is going and coming up with ways that make everyone work better. Suddenly it becomes important to them.

Yet changing someone's mindset about something is not easy or a simple process. And it's not just about throwing buzzwords or "-ities". In fact, even terms like "mindset" and "motivation" can be a turnoff to many people - including me. Human beings are complex: thoughts affect emotions and behaviors, and your emotions and behaviors will affect the thoughts again. And it's not as simple as changing how you perceive something. And importantly, **If something is truly useless, no matter how one tries to think of it positively, it would shatter when it faces the real world**.

## You Don't Have to Love it.
We often try to seek perfection. This idea that we need to be passionate about what we do is deeply embedded in many of us. We don't need to love everything we do - that's already the life of most people on the planet. It's rare to find someone loving their work. And even if they do, just ask them. How much of it do you like? Ask a teacher: do they love every moment in teaching? Of course not. Then ask a programmer: yeah, it's fun sometimes when finally an annoying bug is fixed, or when "all tests pass". But it's not always fun. And that's totally fine. 

*If we only did things out of passion, society is doomed*. 

But we can do better, and we deserve better. It's just feels sometimes that we don't care enough about what we claim we care about.
